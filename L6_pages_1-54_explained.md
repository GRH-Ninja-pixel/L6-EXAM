# Unit 9 - Only Time Will Tell

## Main idea

This unit is about how life changes over time. The activities compare the **past, present, and future** and ask students to think about social and technological changes.

Examples from the unit include changes in:

- education: printed books -> e-books -> virtual classrooms;
- communication: people meeting in person -> communicating online;
- work: assembly lines -> multiple workplaces -> intelligent robots;
- transportation: steam trains -> non-polluting vehicles -> driverless cars;
- shopping and entertainment: older local businesses -> modern digital services.

The important skill is learning to describe a change and then predict what may happen next.

## Grammar: past, present, and future time expressions

The unit contrasts structures such as:

- **A few years ago, there were ...**
- **These days, they are building ...**
- **Soon, there will be ...**
- **We used to ...**
- **Nowadays, ...**
- **We are going to ...**
- **might ...** for a possible future.

### `Used to`

Use **used to + base verb** for a repeated past situation or habit that is no longer true.

- We **used to go** to a burger place after class.
- Kids **used to hang out** with friends after school.

The key meaning is: **this was true in the past, but it is different now.**

### Future forms

The unit uses several ways to talk about the future:

- **will** for a future fact or prediction;
- **be going to** for a planned/intended future;
- **might** for a less certain possibility.

Compare:

- There **will** be more apartment blocks.
- We **are going to** have virtual friends.
- Restaurants **might not** exist in the future.

## Pronunciation

The unit also practices **intonation in statements beginning with time phrases**.

For example:

- **In the past,** few women went to college.
- **Today,** there are more women than men in college.
- **In the future,** women all over the world will go to college.

The time phrase sets the context, and the rest of the sentence carries the main information.

## Grammar: first conditional / possible consequences

The unit then moves to cause and effect:

> **If + present simple, will/may/might + base verb**

Examples:

- If you get a high-paying job, you **will have** more cash to spend.
- If you have more cash, you **will be able to** buy more things.
- If you don't save your money, you **may have to** get a weekend job.
- If you have a weekend job, you **might not have** much free time.

This describes a **real or possible situation** and its likely consequence.

### Important point

The `if` clause normally uses the **present simple**, even though the consequence is about the future.

- Correct: **If I study, I will pass.**
- Not: *If I will study, I will pass.*

## Vocabulary: collocations

The unit practices common word combinations, for example:

- join **a club / a gym**;
- spend **money / time**;
- feel **tired / stressed / jealous**;
- earn **a living / your own money**;
- get **a degree / work experience**;
- keep **in shape**.

Learning these as combinations is more useful than learning each word separately.

## Reading: Fighting for a Future

The reading describes **Aguaviva**, a small village in northern Spain, and its mayor **Luis Bricio**.

The village was losing population because young people left for cities. By 1991 only 618 people remained, and many residents were elderly. Houses were empty, the school had few children, and the local future looked weak.

In 2000, Luis Bricio tried to reverse the decline. He advertised abroad and offered families:

- a home;
- a job;
- free health care;
- education for their children for at least five years.

Families came from places such as Buenos Aires and Romania. The school population grew, the local economy improved, old houses were repaired, and a factory opened.

The new families also faced problems, including:

- adapting from a large city to a small village;
- limited public transportation;
- learning a new language;
- missing family and friends back home.

### Main lesson from the reading

A place can change when people introduce a practical solution to a serious social problem. The reading also shows that a new opportunity can involve both **advantages and difficulties**.

# Unit 10 - I Like Working with People

## Main idea

This unit focuses on **work, jobs, personal qualities, and career choices**. Students talk about what they enjoy, what they dislike, and what kind of job fits their abilities.

## 21st-century skills

The unit introduces skills such as:

- creativity;
- information fluency;
- communication;
- collaboration;
- flexibility;
- responsibility;
- critical thinking;
- analyzing and solving problems;
- developing new ideas;
- learning new things;
- teaching others.

The central question is not only **"What job do you want?"**, but also **"What skills do you have that make you suitable for that job?"**

## Grammar: gerunds

A **gerund** is an `-ing` form used like a noun.

Examples:

- I **love playing** video games.
- I **hate working** on weekends.
- I'm good at **solving** problems.
- I'm interested in **learning** new skills.
- I don't mind **working** evenings.
- I can't stand **commuting**.

After several common verbs and expressions, the activity uses the gerund:

- enjoy + `-ing`
- like + `-ing`
- love + `-ing`
- hate + `-ing`
- don't mind + `-ing`
- be good at + `-ing`
- be interested in + `-ing`
- can't stand + `-ing`

## Short responses

The unit practices short responses for agreement/disagreement:

### With affirmative statements

- "I love playing video games."
- "**So do I.**"

- "I'm good at solving problems."
- "**So am I.**"

### With negative statements

- "I don't mind working evenings."
- "**Neither do I.**"

- "I'm not good at selling."
- "**Neither am I.**"

- "I can't stand commuting."
- "**Neither can I.**"

The helping verb changes to match the original sentence (`do`, `am`, `can`, etc.).

## Career choice

The unit asks students to match jobs with personal preferences. For example:

- someone who enjoys dealing with the public may fit a customer-facing job;
- someone who likes solving problems may fit an analytical job;
- someone who dislikes working alone may prefer teamwork;
- someone who hates weekends may avoid jobs that require weekend shifts.

The goal is to connect **personality + skills + preferences -> suitable career**.

## Vocabulary: personality traits

Positive examples include:

- creative;
- level-headed;
- efficient;
- punctual;
- reliable;
- generous;
- hardworking.

Negative examples include:

- impatient;
- critical;
- disorganized;
- moody;
- forgetful;
- short-tempered;
- strict.

## Grammar: `because` for reasons

Use **because** to introduce a reason.

- I'd make a good journalist **because I'm good at writing**.
- I wouldn't want to be a teacher **because I'm very impatient**.
- I could be a flight attendant **because I really enjoy traveling**.

A useful pattern is:

> **I could / couldn't / would / wouldn't ... because ...**

This lets you explain why a job is or is not suitable for you.

## Writing skill

The unit includes an **online cover letter** for a job application. A good short cover letter:

1. identifies the job opening;
2. expresses interest;
3. explains why the applicant is suitable;
4. mentions relevant experience or skills;
5. asks for an interview or next step.

# Unit 11 - It's Really Worth Seeing!

## Main idea

This unit is about **interesting places, famous landmarks, travel, and things that are worth seeing**.

The reading and listening activities discuss famous attractions and information about places around the world.

## Grammar: passive voice with the simple past

The key grammar point is the **simple past passive**.

### Active

> The Disney brothers **founded** the company in 1923.

### Passive

> The company **was founded** by the Disney brothers in 1923.

The passive changes the focus from **who did the action** to **what happened to the object**.

### Form

> **subject + was/were + past participle**

Examples:

- The park **was opened** in 1955.
- The film **was directed** by ...
- The building **was designed** by ...

### When to use the passive

Use it when:

- the doer is unknown;
- the doer is not important;
- the event/result is more important than the person who did it.

The reading style in this unit uses passive forms because descriptions of landmarks often focus on **when something was built, opened, designed, or created**.

# Unit 12 - It's a Long Story

## Main idea

This unit deals with **events and experiences that have been happening over a period of time**. It also uses stories and examples involving unusual events and coincidences.

## Grammar: present perfect continuous

The main structure is:

> **have/has + been + verb-ing**

Use it for an activity that **started in the past and continues into the present**, especially when the duration is important.

Examples from the lesson pattern:

- I've **been working** two jobs.
- I've **been studying** since January.
- I've **been trying** to save money.
- I've **been taking** courses.

Questions use:

- **How long have you been ... ?**
- **What have you been doing lately?**

### Present perfect vs. present perfect continuous

The continuous form emphasizes the **activity and its duration**.

Compare:

- I have worked here for five years. -> fact/experience;
- I have been working here for five years. -> emphasis on the ongoing activity.

Common time expressions include:

- **for** + length of time: for five years;
- **since** + starting point: since 2021;
- **lately / recently** for recent activity.

## Communication goal

The unit helps you talk naturally about what you have been doing recently, especially when meeting someone you have not seen for a while.

# Unit 13 - That's Entertainment!

## Main idea

This unit focuses on **movies, television, shows, reviews, and opinions about entertainment**.

Students discuss what they like or dislike, compare forms of entertainment, and evaluate movies.

## Grammar: participles as adjectives

Both present and past participles can describe feelings or things.

### Present participle (`-ing`)

This often describes the **thing/person that causes the feeling**.

- The movie is **interesting**.
- The special effects are **amazing**.
- The story is **confusing**.

### Past participle (`-ed`)

This often describes the **person who experiences the feeling**.

- I'm **interested** in action movies.
- I'm **fascinated** by the story.
- I'm **confused** by the ending.

### Easy way to remember

> **-ing = causing the feeling**
>
> **-ed = experiencing the feeling**

Compare:

- The movie is **boring**. -> It causes boredom.
- I am **bored**. -> I experience boredom.

## Vocabulary and opinions

The unit uses descriptive words for entertainment, including groups such as:

- amazing / awful;
- bizarre / weird;
- fantastic / wonderful;
- horrible / terrible;
- interesting / fascinating;
- odd / strange;
- silly / ridiculous.

The skill is to choose vocabulary with the right **strength of opinion**.

## Reading: The Real Art of Acting

The reading presents ideas about acting and performance, using examples to discuss what makes an actor effective.

The broader lesson is to support opinions with **specific reasons and examples**, rather than only saying that something is good or bad.

# Unit 14 - Now I Get It!

## Main idea

This unit is about **communication, understanding, gestures, and rules/regulations**.

It highlights the fact that the same gesture can have different meanings in different cultures or situations.

## Grammar: modals + adverbs

The unit combines modal verbs with adverbs to express different degrees of possibility or certainty.

### Modals

- **might**: a possibility;
- **could**: another possible interpretation;
- **must**: strong certainty.

Examples:

- It **might** mean that someone wants to accept.
- It **could** mean that the person doesn't want to do anything.
- That **must** mean "no."

### Adverbs

Adverbs can strengthen or weaken the statement:

- **maybe / perhaps** -> less certain;
- **probably** -> fairly strong expectation;
- **definitely** -> very strong certainty.

Compare:

- Maybe she wants to accept.
- She probably doesn't want to accept.
- She definitely means "no."

## Rules and regulations

The unit also deals with signs and social rules, such as:

- prohibited activities;
- required actions;
- restrictions in public places;
- safety signs and regulations.

The important communication skill is being able to **interpret a sign or gesture and explain what it probably means**.

# Unit 15 - I Wouldn't Have Done That

## Main idea

This unit focuses on **imaginary situations, alternative choices, and what someone would do in a situation that is not presented as real**. It encourages people to explain what they would choose and why.

## Grammar: unreal conditional sentences with `if`

The grammar page uses the **unreal conditional** for imaginary situations.

### Form

> **If + simple past, would/could/might + base verb**

The textbook example is: **If I found $40,000, I would keep it.**

Other possibilities use: 

- **could + base verb** for an imagined option or ability;
- **might + base verb** for an imagined possibility.

Examples following the same pattern:

- If I **won** a lot of money, I **would travel**.
- If I **had** more free time, I **could take** another course.
- If I **moved** abroad, I **might learn** a new language.

### Meaning

The speaker is imagining a situation rather than describing a normal real-life fact. The `if` clause sets up the imaginary condition; the second clause gives the imagined result.

The unit title, **I wouldn't have done that**, also leads into discussion of past decisions and whether another choice would have been better, but the main grammar presentation on the page uses the **simple past + would/could/might + base verb** pattern above.


## Vocabulary and discussion

The topic encourages opinions such as:

- what someone should have done;
- what you would have done in the same situation;
- whether a past decision was a good or bad choice.

The language is strongly connected to **regret and evaluation**.

# Unit 16 - Making Excuses

## Main idea

The final pages in the requested range are about **making excuses, giving reasons for not doing something, and reporting other people's words**.

The unit contrasts good and poor excuses and practices explaining what someone asked or said.

## Grammar: reported speech for requests

Reported speech changes a direct request into something that reports the request.

### Direct request

> "Arrive on time for the meeting."

### Reported request

> She **said to me to arrive** on time for the meeting.

More natural textbook patterns also include:

> She **told me to** arrive on time.
>
> She **asked me to** pick up some food.
>
> She **told me not to** leave my bike in the hallway.

### Core structures

#### `tell + person + to + verb`

- He **told me to** leave.
- She **told us to** be careful.

#### `ask + person + to + verb`

- He **asked me to** help.
- She **asked him to** call later.

#### Negative request

Use:

> **told/asked + person + not to + verb**

Example:

- She **told me not to** leave my bike in the hallway.

## Grammar: reported statements

The later part of the unit changes direct statements into reported statements.

Example pattern:

### Direct

> "I'm not feeling well."

### Reported

> She said that she **wasn't feeling well**.

Another example:

### Direct

> "I have homework for the weekend."

### Reported

> She said that she **had** homework for the weekend.

The lesson therefore introduces the common idea of **backshifting**: when we report a statement from the past, the verb often moves one step back in tense.

Examples:

- am/is -> was;
- are -> were;
- have/has -> had;
- will -> would;
- can -> could.

## Important difference: `say` vs. `tell`

A useful rule is:

- **say + statement** -> She said she was tired.
- **tell + person + statement** -> She told me she was tired.

Do not use *tell* without a person in this pattern.

## Making excuses

The speaking and listening activities ask students to identify excuses and decide whether they sound believable or not.

A useful excuse normally gives:

1. a reason;
2. enough detail to make the reason understandable;
3. a connection between the reason and the missed action.

The unit also encourages students to think critically about whether an excuse is a **real explanation or simply a weak excuse**.

## Reading on page 54: Day Off Work

The reading continues the unit's theme of taking time away from work and dealing with excuses or workplace situations.

The key study skill is to understand the difference between:

- a genuine reason for absence;
- an excuse that sounds weak or unbelievable;
- an explanation that gives enough context to make the situation clear.

# Most important grammar to memorize

## 1. `Used to`

> **used to + base verb**

Past habit or state that is no longer true.

**Example:** I used to play outside every day.

## 2. First conditional

> **If + present simple, will/may/might + base verb**

Real or possible future consequence.

**Example:** If I study, I will pass.

## 3. Gerunds

> **verb/expression + verb-ing**

Used after expressions such as enjoy, hate, don't mind, be good at, and be interested in.

**Example:** I enjoy learning languages.

## 4. Simple past passive

> **was/were + past participle**

Focus on the action/result rather than the doer.

**Example:** The building was designed in 1950.

## 5. Present perfect continuous

> **have/has + been + verb-ing**

An activity that began in the past and continues into the present or has a strong connection to the present.

**Example:** I have been studying for three hours.

## 6. `-ing` vs. `-ed` adjectives

- **interesting** = causes interest;
- **interested** = feels interest.

- **boring** = causes boredom;
- **bored** = feels boredom.

## 7. Modals + adverbs

Use them to show degree of certainty:

- might / maybe -> possible;
- probably -> likely;
- definitely / must -> strong certainty.

## 8. Unreal conditional

For an imaginary situation:

> **If + simple past, would/could/might + base verb**

**Example:** If I found $40,000, I would keep it.

## 9. Reported requests

> **tell/ask + person + (not) to + verb**

**Example:** She asked me to call her.

## 10. Reported statements

> **say/tell + reported clause**

**Example:** He said that he was tired.

# Final study strategy

For an exam, focus first on the **grammar formulas and their meanings**, then learn the vocabulary in **phrases/collocations**, and finally practice producing your own sentences.

A good self-test is to explain each grammar point without looking at the notes:

1. What does `used to` mean?
2. How is the first conditional formed?
3. Why do we use a gerund after `enjoy`?
4. How do you form the simple past passive?
5. What is the difference between present perfect and present perfect continuous?
6. What is the difference between `interesting` and `interested`?
7. How do `might`, `probably`, and `definitely` change certainty?
8. How do you talk about an unreal past situation?
9. How do you report a request?
10. How do `say` and `tell` differ?

These are the core ideas that connect the material across pages 1-54.
